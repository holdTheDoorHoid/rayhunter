# Is This For Me?

Most tools want you to use them. This page is trying to do something different:
help you decide honestly whether Rayhunter addresses a risk you actually have.
For some people it genuinely does. For a lot of people it is fascinating but
not protective, and you are better served knowing that now than discovering it
after weeks of effort.

So this page does not sell. It lays out who tends to get real value from
Rayhunter and who does not, and lets you place yourself.

## Start with the honest baseline

Two things are true at once, and holding both is the key to this whole page.

Cell-site simulators are real, they are used, and the surveillance they enable
can have serious consequences for the right person in the wrong place.

And: for the average person going about an average day, the odds of being
targeted by one are low, Rayhunter cannot protect you even when you are
targeted, and a warning it raises will more often be a quirk of the network
than an attack. [What This Tool Does](./what-this-does.md) explains why it
watches rather than defends, and [Reading Warnings Without
Panicking](./concepts/interpreting-warnings.md) explains why most single
warnings are not attacks.

Neither of those cancels the other. The question is not "are these devices
dangerous" (they can be) but "does watching for them change anything for *me*."

## Where Rayhunter earns its place

Rayhunter tends to be genuinely useful when three things are true together:
you have a **specific, credible reason** to think you might be near this kind
of surveillance; you are willing to **learn to read what it tells you** rather
than react to every warning; and you value **evidence you can preserve and
share** over a sense of personal safety it cannot provide. The clearest fits:

- **Journalists**, especially those covering protests, security, organized
  crime, or working with sensitive sources — where knowing whether surveillance
  equipment was present at a place and time is part of the story, and a
  preserved recording is part of the reporting.
- **Organizers and activists**, particularly around protests and actions,
  where cell-site simulators have a documented history of use and the question
  "was this crowd being monitored" is a real one. EFF's guide to cell-phone
  surveillance at protests ([Sources and Further Reading](./references.md)) is
  worth reading alongside this, because it is about the whole threat, of which
  Rayhunter addresses one part.
- **Researchers and technologists** studying how these devices work or mapping
  where they appear. Rayhunter was built partly to feed exactly this kind of
  distributed, public knowledge, and here its evidence-gathering strength is
  the whole point.
- **People with a concrete, individual reason** to believe they may be
  targeted — some human-rights defenders, some people in specific legal or
  personal situations. If you are in this group you likely already know it, and
  the [risk](./concepts/risk.md) and [interpretation](./concepts/interpreting-warnings.md)
  pages matter especially for you.

Notice what these have in common: a reason that is specific rather than
general, and a use for evidence rather than a need for protection. That is the
profile Rayhunter fits.

## Where it is interesting but not protective

For many thoughtful, privacy-conscious people, the honest answer is that
Rayhunter is a fascinating instrument that will not meaningfully change their
safety. There is nothing wrong with using it anyway — curiosity is a fine
reason, and more eyes on the problem has real public value. But go in clear:

- **If your worry is general rather than specific** — "surveillance is
  everywhere and I want to be safe" — Rayhunter will not deliver safety. It
  watches one narrow threat, on one device, and defends against none of it. Your
  privacy is better served by the ordinary measures: strong device security,
  encrypted messaging, and the platform settings that can turn off the old
  networks these attacks rely on ([Sources and Further Reading](./references.md)
  covers those).
- **If you would read every warning as proof you are being watched**, Rayhunter
  may cost you more peace than it gives, because most warnings are not that.
  This is a real consideration, not a small one — the tool asks for a certain
  steadiness in how you read it.
- **If you want a device that protects your phone**, this is not that device,
  and no amount of configuration makes it one.

Choosing not to use Rayhunter after reading this is a perfectly good outcome of
this page. It means it answered its question.

## A way to place yourself

Ask, in order:

1. **Do I have a specific reason** — a role, an activity, a situation — to
   think I might be near a cell-site simulator? Not "could anyone," but "is
   there something particular about me or what I do."
2. **Do I want evidence, or do I want protection?** Rayhunter offers the first
   and not the second. If you need the second, this is the wrong tool and it is
   better to know now.
3. **Will I put in the time to read it well?** Its value depends entirely on
   interpreting warnings correctly. Used without that, it produces anxiety, not
   insight.

Three yeses means Rayhunter probably fits, and the [Quick
Start](./quick-start.md) is your next stop. A no at question 1 or 2, and it is
likely interesting-but-not-protective for you — still fine to explore, as long
as you hold that framing.
